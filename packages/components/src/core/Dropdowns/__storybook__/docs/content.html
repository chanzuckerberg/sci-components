Dropdowns

Seven components open a list of options from something the user clicked, and most of them are made out of the others. This page is the map.

The short version. Reach for Dropdown when the user is picking a value, Menu when they are choosing a command, and ComplexFilter when they are narrowing a set of results. The other four are the parts those three are assembled from, and are worth reaching for only when none of the three is shaped the way you need.

Which one do I use

Component

Reach for it when

What it gives you

Menu

The choices are commands, such as Rename, Duplicate or Log out, or a short list of options that the trigger does not have to report back.

The simplest of the family: a surface anchored to your trigger, holding the rows you write into it yourself.

Dropdown

The user is picking a value, and the trigger should then show what they picked. This is the one to start from.

The whole arrangement: a trigger, a searchable list, single or multiple selection, sections, and optional Apply and Cancel buttons.

ComplexFilter

The selection is a filter over something else on the page, and the user needs to see what they have applied.

A Dropdown that lists the selection underneath itself as tags, each one removable on its own.

DropdownMenu

You want Dropdown's list but not its trigger, because the menu opens from something else: a button, an icon, a table cell.

The list in a popper, positioned against whatever element you hand it, with the open state left to you.

InputDropdown

You want Dropdown's trigger without its menu, usually to anchor a DropdownMenu you are driving yourself.

The trigger alone: a button styled as a field, showing a label and either the current value or a count.

Autocomplete

The list has to sit somewhere a popper cannot go: inside a panel, a dialog body, or a layout you are placing yourself.

The list on its own, with search, sections, multiple selection and multiple columns, and no popper or trigger around it.

MenuItem

You are writing the rows of a Menu, or replacing the row every other component here draws for you.

One row, with its checkmark, icon, count and details, in the states a row can be in.

How they fit together

Everything above is built out of the two components at the bottom of this list, one layer adding one thing to the layer beneath it.

  1. MenuItem is a single row, and nothing more. It is what a Menu is filled with, and what the list below draws for each of its options.

  2. Autocomplete is the list: the rows, the search field, the sections, the checkmarks, and the columns when there is more than one. It has no surface of its own to float above the page, and nothing to open it.

  3. DropdownMenu puts that list in a popper and positions it against an element you name. It still has no trigger: you say what it is anchored to and whether it is open.

  4. InputDropdown is the missing trigger, and is useful by itself for exactly that reason. It reads as a form field and reports the selection back: a value for a single choice, a count for several.

  5. Dropdown is the two of them wired together, and takes on what sits between them: opening and closing, holding the selection, and the Apply and Cancel buttons when the choice should not take effect until it is confirmed.

  6. ComplexFilter is a Dropdown that also shows its selection as a tag apiece beneath the trigger, so a filter that has been applied stays visible next to the results it narrowed.

Menu sits outside that stack. It is the Science Design System restyling of Material UI's own Menu, and shares only MenuItem with the rest.

Menu or DropdownMenu

The names are close together and the components are not, which is the single most common mix-up in this family.

Menu is for doing something. Its rows are commands, you write them yourself as MenuItems, and picking one runs an action and closes the surface. Nothing is remembered afterwards, and the trigger goes on saying whatever it said before.

DropdownMenu is for choosing something. Its rows come from an options array rather than from you, it can search and group and hold several selections at once, and what the user picks is a value your app keeps. It is the menu half of Dropdown, split out for when the trigger has to be something Dropdown does not draw.

So: a command runs, and a value is kept. If the thing being chosen has to be read back out anywhere afterwards, it is not a Menu.

Building your own

Dropdown is the right starting point, and dropping below it costs you the wiring it was doing. Two reasons are worth it.

The first is a trigger Dropdown does not draw. Pair a DropdownMenu with any element that can hand over a DOM node to be anchored against, such as a button, an icon or a cell in a table, and keep the open state beside it. InputDropdown is only the default trigger, not a required one.

The second is a list that cannot float. A popper is positioned against the window, which makes it the wrong thing to put inside a panel or a dialog body that scrolls independently. Use Autocomplete directly there and lay it out yourself.
